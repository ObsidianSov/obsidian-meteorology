# Trend analysis: method

Implemented in `trend_stats.js`. Status: experimental until validated on station data
(Milestone 4).

## What is calculated

For an annual series (rainfall total, mean temperature, onset day):

1. **Mann-Kendall test** (Mann 1945; Kendall 1975): a rank-based test of whether the
   series tends to rise or fall over time. It uses the statistic S, its variance with a
   correction for tied values, a continuity-corrected Z score, and a two-sided p-value.
2. **Sen's slope** (Sen 1968): the median of all pairwise slopes between years, with a
   95% range from the rank method in Sen (1968), the same rule as `scipy.stats.theilslopes`.

Both are standard and robust choices for annual climate series: they are not distorted by one
extreme year the way a least-squares line can be.

## When the app says "trend"

- At least **10 usable years** are required (project choice: the normal approximation behind
  the p-value is not reliable on very short series). Fewer years: "too few years to test".
- A trend is reported only if **p < 0.05** (conventional 5% level). Otherwise the app says
  "No statistically detectable trend" and still shows the best-estimate slope with its 95% range,
  noting that it cannot be told apart from natural variation.
- The wording is generated by `describeTrend` and tested: it cannot say "increasing" or
  "decreasing" unless the test supports it.

## Verification

Results were compared with independent Python implementations:
`pymannkendall` 1.4.3 (S, variance, Z, p, tau) and `scipy` 1.17.1 (`theilslopes`: slope and 95%
range) on seven reference series, including ties, a constant series and a 7-year series. They
agree to about 1e-9 (p-values to 1e-8). The reference values are stored in `test_trend_stats.js`.

## Unusual years and how much the verdict depends on them

A trend can be produced by a few extreme years, or by a break in the data source. The app
therefore also:

1. **Flags unusual years** with Tukey's fences: a year is unusual if it lies more than
   1.5 x IQR beyond the quartiles of the series (quartiles by linear interpolation, as in
   `numpy.percentile`). Needs at least 10 years. Flagged years are never removed from the data
   or from the charts: they may be real extremes.
2. **Re-runs the trend test without the flagged years.** If the verdict changes, the page
   says so, lists the years, and tells the user to check the series against another source.

Real example that motivated this: NASA POWER annual rainfall for Katsina, 1990 to 2019,
ranges from 475 to 891 mm with no trend (p = 0.187). Adding 2020 to 2024 (about 1,000 to
1,780 mm) gives a "significant increase" (p = 0.002). The cause of the high recent values
is not known; a data-source problem is possible.

Limits: the fences are computed from all years, so a cluster of unusual years widens them and
can hide some (in the example, 2020, 2021, 2022 and 2024 are flagged but 2023 is not).
The Pettitt change-point test was considered and not used here: it has little power for a
shift in the last few years of a series. Comparing with a second data source is the real fix
(Milestones 3 and 4).

## What this does NOT establish

- **Cause.** A significant trend describes this dataset at this location. It does not say why.
- **Independence.** Mann-Kendall assumes the yearly values are independent. If successive
  years are correlated (autocorrelation), the test can find trends that are not real. No
  correction (for example pre-whitening) is applied yet.
- **Shape.** The test detects a steady rise or fall, not changes in variability or step changes.
- **Real-world rainfall.** The data are a gridded estimate (NASA POWER) for one point and
  about 35 years, not a station record.

## The linear regression row

The advanced table still shows the least-squares slope and R² for comparison only. Headline
statements use Mann-Kendall and Sen's slope.

## Onset spread

The percentage "coefficient of variation" for onset is no longer shown: it depends on counting
days from 1 January, which is arbitrary. The app shows the mean onset as a date and the spread as
a standard deviation in days.
